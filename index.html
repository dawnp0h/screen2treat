Prevention is better than cure. Except for those we failed to reach.

Read the case → Six ethical grounds and seven objections.

The case

I have cervical cancer. What is going to happen to me?

Seen yesterday

Six grounds, drawn from the major ethical traditions, support two duties. The first duty is to prevent cervical cancer through screening and the treatment of precancer. The second duty is to treat cervical cancer in the women prevention failed to reach. No ethical theory justifies neglecting either duty.

Important disclaimerCervical cancer affects women, and also transgender men and non-binary people who have a cervix. This website uses “women” for readability. The obligations it describes extend to each person at risk.

1. Duty

Where harm is foreseeable and avoidable, inaction is a choice.

Persistent HPV infection is a necessary cause of cervical cancer, and HPV is present in 99.7% of cervical carcinomas (Walboomers et al., 1999). Persistent infection takes 15 to 20 years to develop into cancer (WHO, 2024). The interval of 15 to 20 years offers two points of rescue, namely screening every 5 to 10 years from age 30, and treatment of precancer (WHO, 2025). A single screen at age 35, linked to treatment, reduces lifetime cervical cancer risk by approximately 25 to 36% (Goldie et al., 2005). Each death is therefore preventable, not inevitable.

Principle of rescue · PrinciplismSinger argues that a person able to prevent serious harm, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, is obliged to act (Singer, 1972). Principlism calls the same duties beneficence and nonmaleficence (Beauchamp and Childress, 2019). Cervical cancer meets Singer's test. The harm is death, and the sacrifice is a screening test and an outpatient treatment.

2. Justice

Geography and income should not decide who survives.

Of the 348,709 women who died of cervical cancer in 2022 (Wu et al., 2025), approximately 94% lived in low and middle income countries (WHO, 2025). Among women aged 30 to 49, 84% in high income countries have ever been screened, against 9% in lower middle income and 11% in low income countries (Bruni et al., 2022). In Kenya, cervical cancer causes 12% of all cancer deaths, compared with 0.64% in the United Kingdom (World Economic Forum, 2024). Women living with HIV face six times the risk (Stelzle et al., 2021). Because cervical cancer follows disadvantage so closely, the persistence of cervical cancer is an injustice, not a misfortune.

Rawlsian justiceRawls holds that social and economic inequalities are just only when the inequalities benefit the least advantaged (Rawls, 1971). Daniels extends Rawls to health. Disease curtails fair equality of opportunity, so a just society must protect every member against preventable disease (Daniels, 2008). A cancer that kills the poorest women at the highest rates fails Rawls's and Daniels's tests alike.

3. Rights

Health, and the benefits of science, belong to everyone.

Article 25 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights recognises the right of everyone to a standard of living adequate for health, including medical care, and Article 27 the right to share in scientific advancement and its benefits (United Nations, 1948). Article 12 of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights recognises the right of everyone to the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health, and Article 15 recognises the right to enjoy the benefits of scientific progress (United Nations, 1966). Article 12 of the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women requires states to eliminate discrimination against women in health care and to ensure equal access to health services (United Nations, 1979). Article 24 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child recognises the right of every child to the highest attainable standard of health and requires states to develop preventive health care (United Nations, 1989).

The Declaration of Alma-Ata affirmed health as a fundamental human right and called for health for all (WHO and UNICEF, 1978). Withholding proven screening and treatment because of price, distance or neglect denies that right. In 2020, 194 countries committed to eliminating cervical cancer, the first such pledge for any cancer (WHO, 2020b), so the obligation is now each government's own commitment.

Research ethics carries the same obligation into the trials that built the evidence. The Belmont Report requires that the benefits and burdens of research be distributed justly (National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research, 1979). The Declaration of Helsinki requires researchers to consider how benefits, risks and burdens are distributed, because research takes place amid structural inequities (World Medical Association, 2024). Women who bear the risks of research are owed access to what that research proves.

Kantian deontology · Human rightsKant requires that every person be treated as an end, never merely as a means (Kant, 1785). Withholding proven screening and treatment on grounds of cost or distance treats a woman's life as a cost to be weighed, not as an end in itself. These treaties and declarations convert Kant's duty into obligations that states have accepted.

4. Legacy

The deaths fall on families. Screening and treatment keep mothers alive.

Of the 1,047,000 children newly orphaned by maternal cancer in 2020, 210,000, or 20%, lost their mother to cervical cancer (Guida et al., 2022). Screening a woman and treating what is found protects her, and protects the families and communities that depend on her.

Ethics of care · Capabilities approachCare ethics grounds moral obligation in relationships of dependence (Held, 2006). The capabilities approach counts life and bodily health among the central capabilities every just society must secure (Nussbaum, 2011). A mother's death removes the care on which a child depends, narrows each child's own capabilities, and takes a worker, carer and citizen from her community.

5. Stewardship

Scarce resources demand the best possible use.

Screening once at age 35, linked to treatment, costs less than US$500 per year of life saved in India, Kenya, Peru, South Africa and Thailand (Goldie et al., 2005). Delay does not save money. Without change, cases are projected to rise by 56.8%, and deaths by 80.7%, by 2050 (Wu et al., 2025). Screening is the prudent choice, and treatment for women already ill is the necessary partner of screening.

UtilitarianismUtilitarianism judges an action by the total welfare the action produces (Mill, 1863). Screening linked to treatment, at less than US$500 per year of life saved (Goldie et al., 2005), maximises welfare on utilitarian terms.

6. Those we failed to reach

Screening finds disease early. Treatment saves women alive today.

Screening without treatment saves no one. In 78 low and lower middle income countries, twice lifetime screening with cancer treatment would avert approximately 300,000 deaths by 2030. The cancer treatment in that model is surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy (Canfell et al., 2020). Women beyond cure are owed palliative care. Cancer case fatality is approximately 75% in low income countries, against 46% in high income countries (Farmer et al., 2010). See treatment capacity.

Resource allocation that expands screening without matching investment in treatment risks violating each woman's claim to justice. The third WHO target, 90% of women with precancer treated and 90% of women with invasive cancer managed, makes treatment part of elimination itself (WHO, 2020b).

The third Wilson and Jungner criterion requires facilities for diagnosis and treatment to be available (Wilson and Jungner, 1968). A screening programme without treatment therefore fails the classic test of screening itself. See the screening criteria. WHO's screen and treat model closes the gap by linking a positive test directly to treatment. See the screen-to-treatment pathway.

Four principlesGillon's four principles of respect for autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence and justice ask whether each woman is treated as a full moral agent, entitled to treatment and not merely to a diagnosis (Gillon, 1994).

Deontology and consequentialismBroad distinguishes deontological duty from consequentialist evaluation (Broad, 2014). A health system that screens but cannot treat may satisfy a procedural duty to offer testing, yet fail the consequentialist standard of improved outcomes. A health system without treatment exposes women to the harms of a positive result with no pathway to care.

Distributive justiceRoemer distinguishes equality of resources from equality of opportunity, and argues that justice requires compensation for circumstances beyond individual control, such as geography and income (Roemer, 1996). Cook and Hegtvedt distinguish equity from equality (Cook and Hegtvedt, 1983). Equal provision of screening is not equitable when treatment capacity is distributed unevenly, because equity requires resources to follow need, not uniform input.

Conclusion. Consequentialism, deontology, contractualism, rights theory, care ethics and distributive justice reach the same conclusion. Elimination is not charity but an obligation every government has already accepted. Prevention is better than cure. For the women prevention failed to reach, cure is the obligation that remains.
“Eliminating any cancer would have once seemed an impossible dream.” Dr Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus, World Health Organization Director-General (2017 to 2026) (WHO, 2020b)
Consider the objections

Common objections

But…

Delivered

The objections most often raised against the screening-to-treatment pillar of cervical cancer elimination.

"Elimination is impossible. No cancer has ever been eliminated."

WHO defines elimination as fewer than 4 new cases per 100,000 women each year in every country, not zero cases. In 2020, 194 countries committed to reaching that threshold (WHO, 2020b).

Example · Australia

In 2021, Australia recorded no cervical cancer in women under 25, a first since records began in 1982, and Australia is on course for elimination by 2035 (Liotta, 2025), as modelling projected (Hall et al., 2019). Australia replaced the Pap test with five-yearly HPV testing in 2017, and the projection depends on sustaining high screening participation (Hall et al., 2019). Elimination, once begun, must be sustained.

"We're not going to make it. The 2030 targets will be missed."

The 2030 targets are milestones, not the deadline for elimination. The Global Strategy asks each country to reach them by 2030 to be on course to eliminate cervical cancer within the next century (WHO, 2020a). A missed milestone does not make elimination impossible. A missed milestone makes each year of delay more costly. Without change, cervical cancer deaths are projected to rise by 80.7% by 2050 (Wu et al., 2025). Each woman screened and treated counts towards elimination, whether or not her country reaches 70% by 2030.

"Vaccinate the girls and move on. Treating women who are already ill costs too much."

Vaccination protects girls before infection but cannot help women already infected or ill. In 78 low and lower middle income countries, vaccination alone would reduce cervical cancer mortality by 0.1% by 2030, whereas adding twice lifetime screening and treatment would reduce mortality by 34.2% and avert 300,000 deaths (Canfell et al., 2020). Elimination depends on each subsequent step. Vaccination prevents infection, screening finds the disease vaccination did not prevent, and treatment cures what screening finds. Nor is treatment a poor investment. Every dollar invested in vaccination, screening and treatment returns US$3.20, or US$26 counting wider health benefits (WHO, 2020b). Neglect only defers the cost of cervical cancer and increases it.

"Elimination tramples personal choice."

Respect for autonomy requires informed, voluntary choice (Beauchamp and Childress, 2019). Elimination demands access, not compulsion. The aim of elimination is that every woman can choose protection, and no woman is denied protection by geography or income.

"Screening women encourages risky sex."

Screening detects disease early. A test result endorses no behaviour, any more than screening for breast or bowel cancer does. Withholding a test that saves lives in order to discourage a behaviour punishes women for an assumption.

"Screening harms healthy women through overtreatment."

Every intervention carries risk, and the remedy is quality, not abstention. Treating precancer stops precancer from progressing to cancer (WHO, 2025). With approximately 94% of cervical cancer deaths already occurring in low and middle income countries (WHO, 2025), the evidence locates the greater harm in women never screened, not in women overtreated. Cervical screening meets nine of the ten Wilson and Jungner criteria. See the screening criteria.

"Cervical cancer is a poor country's problem, not ours."

Moral obligation does not depend on distance. Cervical cancer causes 12% of cancer deaths in Kenya, and 0.64% in the United Kingdom (World Economic Forum, 2024). When a cure reaches some women and not others, the fault lies in how the cure is distributed.

Does cervical screening meet the screening criteria?

Wilson and Jungner set out ten criteria that a disease and a screening programme should meet before screening is justified (Wilson and Jungner, 1968). Where treatment services remain limited, as in many low and middle income countries, cervical screening meets nine of the ten. The one criterion still unmet in many countries, the availability of treatment, is the subject of the sixth ground of the case.

Example · United Kingdom

Written for WHO in 1968, the criteria remain the basis of national screening decisions. In the United Kingdom, the National Screening Committee appraises every proposed programme against criteria built on the original ten. The committee adds that the whole programme, from test to treatment, must be clinically, socially and ethically acceptable, and that staffing and facilities for testing, diagnosis and treatment must be in place before screening begins (UK National Screening Committee, n.d.). Under that standard, a programme that screens without the capacity to treat would not be approved.

  1. The condition is an important health problem.

    348,709 women died of cervical cancer in 2022 (Wu et al., 2025).

  2. An accepted treatment exists.

    Treatment of precancer prevents progression to cancer (WHO, 2025).

  3. Facilities for diagnosis and treatment are available.

    Cancer case fatality is estimated at 75% in low income countries and 46% in high income countries (Farmer et al., 2010).

  4. The disease has a recognisable latent or early stage.

    Precancer precedes cancer by 15 to 20 years (WHO, 2024).

  5. A suitable test exists.

    High performance HPV testing is recommended from age 30 (WHO, 2025).

  6. The test is acceptable to the population.

    In Australia, HPV self collection has proved popular among the most under screened groups (Australian Government Department of Health, 2025).

  7. The natural history of the disease is understood.

    HPV is present in 99.7% of cervical carcinomas (Walboomers et al., 1999).

  8. There is an agreed policy on whom to treat.

    WHO targets treatment for 90% of women with precancer and management for 90% of women with invasive cancer (WHO, 2020b).

  9. The cost of case finding is balanced against overall health spending.

    Every dollar invested returns US$3.20, or US$26 when wider health benefits are counted (WHO, 2020b).

  10. Case finding is a continuing process.

    Women should be screened every 5 to 10 years from age 30, and women living with HIV every 3 to 5 years from age 25 (WHO, 2025).

Screen-to-treatment pathway

WHO recommends a shift to primary high risk HPV DNA testing, in place of visual inspection with acetic acid or cytology (WHO, 2021). HPV DNA testing is simpler, prevents more precancer and cancer, and saves more lives than either method (WHO, 2021). Across four European randomised trials, HPV based screening gave 60 to 70% greater protection against invasive cervical cancer than cytology (Ronco et al., 2014). Samples may be collected by a health care provider or by the woman herself (WHO, 2021).

The patient's journey

  1. 1

    Screening offer and uptake

    Women are invited to organised population screening for the early detection of precancer and cancer, and take a test.

    • HPV DNA testing, with samples collected by a provider or by the woman herself through self-sampling
    • Visual inspection with acetic acid
    • Cytology, by the Papanicolaou (Pap) test

    (WHO, 2021)

  2. 2

    Result communication

    Each person screened receives a result and the next step in care.

  3. 3

    Triage or diagnostic evaluation

    A positive result is assessed by a triage test or by colposcopy, with biopsy of abnormal areas, and is managed by one of three approaches.

    • Screen and treat, with treatment directly after a positive test
    • Screen, triage and treat, with a triage test first
    • Referral for diagnosis before treatment

    (WHO, 2021)

  4. 4

    Treatment of precancer

    Precancerous lesions are treated before cancer develops.

    • Ablation, by thermal ablation or cryotherapy
    • Excision, by large loop excision of the transformation zone, also called the loop electrosurgical excision procedure, or by cold knife conisation

    (WHO, 2021)

  5. 5

    Referral and treatment of invasive cancer

    Women with invasive cancer are referred for treatment, which depends on the extent of disease and on locally available resources.

    • Surgery, including radical hysterectomy and fertility-sparing procedures
    • Radiotherapy, including external beam radiotherapy and brachytherapy
    • Concurrent chemoradiotherapy
    • Systemic therapy, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy and targeted therapy
    • Palliative care

    (Cohen et al., 2019)

  6. 6

    Post-treatment follow-up

    Women are followed after treatment to confirm the treatment has worked.

A patient lost at any of the six steps does not receive the care the pathway is designed to provide.

Both models join screening directly to treatment, so a positive result leads to care, not to a diagnosis alone. Retaining patients and treating women who screen positive rapidly is a fundamental priority for any programme (WHO, 2021).

Three delays framework

The three delays framework began in maternal health. Thaddeus and Maine traced deaths from obstetric complications to delays in deciding to seek care, in reaching a health facility, and in receiving adequate care on arrival (Thaddeus and Maine, 1994). The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery adopted the framework to measure access to surgery, with a target of a capable facility within 2 hours for every patient (Meara et al., 2015). The same three delays stand between a patient and cervical cancer care.

  1. Delay 1

    Seeking care

    A woman must know screening exists and decide to attend. In low income countries, 11% of women aged 30 to 49 have ever been screened (Bruni et al., 2022).

  2. Delay 2

    Reaching care

    A patient must travel to a facility able to provide treatment. In 2020, 26 of 54 African countries had no external beam radiotherapy, so patients in those countries must travel abroad for treatment (Elmore et al., 2021).

  3. Delay 3

    Receiving care

    On arrival, treatment depends on capacity. In low income countries, 5% of patients with cancer can access basic cancer surgery (Sullivan et al., 2015). See treatment capacity.

Treatment capacity

Screening saves lives only where treatment follows a positive result. Surgery, radiotherapy and pain relief are scarcest in the regions where cervical cancer deaths are highest. For women beyond cure, palliative care is the treatment that remains.

5% vs 22%

Patients with cancer able to access basic cancer surgery, in low income versus middle income countries. Over 80% of new cancer cases need surgery (Sullivan et al., 2015).

9 in 10

People in low income countries who cannot access radiotherapy (Atun et al., 2015). In 2020, radiotherapy met 6% to 9% of need in Central, Western and Eastern Africa, against 71% in Northern Africa (Elmore et al., 2021).

0.1 of 298.5

Metric tonnes of morphine-equivalent opioids distributed to low income countries each year, out of 298.5 tonnes distributed worldwide. Morphine is an essential medicine for severe pain. A 10 mg dose costs 3 US cents in high income countries, yet 16 cents in low income countries where available (Knaul et al., 2018).

Price is not the main barrier. Off-patent morphine offers manufacturers little profit, and unduly restrictive drug control laws limit medical use (Knaul et al., 2018). Weak supply systems, absent palliative care policies, limited training and clinicians' fear of legal sanctions deepen the shortage (Lohman et al., 2010).

84%

Women dying of cervical cancer with moderate or severe financial distress. More than 40% of women with advanced cervical cancer are abandoned by their intimate partners (Krakauer et al., 2021).

In 2017, more than 250,000 women dying of cervical cancer and 2.5 million women living with the disease needed palliative care (Krakauer et al., 2021).

The path to elimination by 2030

In August 2020, the World Health Assembly adopted the Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer as a Public Health Problem (WHO, 2020a), the first global commitment to eliminate a cancer (WHO, 2020b). The strategy defines elimination as fewer than 4 cases per 100,000 women a year, and sets three targets that every country must meet by 2030 to be on the path to elimination (WHO, 2020a).

The 90, 70, 90 targets for 2030

The three targets follow the three pillars of the strategy, namely vaccination, screening with treatment of precancer, and management of invasive cancer. A high performance test is one that performs as well as, or better than, an HPV test. Each country is to reach all three targets by 2030, which the strategy projects would set every country on course to eliminate cervical cancer within the next century (WHO, 2020a).

Targets of the WHO Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer as a Public Health Problem (WHO, 2020a). Our Mission

Screen to Treat is making the ethical case to end cervical cancer.

This site sets out six ethical grounds for eliminating cervical cancer, responses to the seven objections most often raised against elimination, and the latest evidence on the pathway from screening to treatment. Each claim is supported by published peer-reviewed research, fact sheets from verified websites, and/or established ethical theories.
